I designed and built Germanly alone, from the first interview to the redesign. It now teaches 120+ learners, and a 20 person pilot study, now under review at CHI, showed learners felt more confident and treated it as something they own.

CHAPTER 01The streak

Every morning I kept the flame alive. Every afternoon German won.

I moved to Siegen for my HCI Master's. Flat viewings, the Ausländerbehörde, the doctor, the bakery: every one assumed I could hold a basic conversation. I did what everyone around me did. I opened Duolingo, kept the streak, and still froze at the counter.

My setup looked like everyone's I later spoke to: fourteen browser tabs of YouTube lessons and grammar blogs, three half finished apps each good at one thing, and a folder of PDFs I never opened twice.

The apps were not broken. They were doing exactly what they were designed to do. That was the problem, and it took me a while to see it.

CHAPTER 02Why people fail

Apps built for retention measure whether you came back. Not whether you can speak.

A retention loop is short and satisfying: a reminder, a few taps, a reward, a streak. It is excellent at bringing people back. But recognising a word on a tile is not the same as recalling it at a counter, and nothing in the loop checks the difference.

The retention loop

Optimised for coming back

Each step is easy, so the loop is fast. The learner rarely produces a sentence, rarely meets an article under pressure, and never learns whether they are ready for an exam.

The learning loop

Optimised for what sticks

Each step asks for a little more effort: recall instead of recognition, a sentence instead of a tile, a readiness score instead of a flame. It is slower, and it is what the exam and real life measure.

    CHAPTER 08Did it work

    Learners stopped renting their German. They started owning it.

    Duolingo is excellent at bringing people back, and I never set out to beat it at that. I tested something different: what happens to confidence, motivation and commitment when learning lives in one path you paid for once. Twenty learners used Germanly for two to four weeks and were interviewed before and after.

    What the pilot shows

    Confidence and self directed motivation rose, scattered tools consolidated, and learners' pricing beliefs flipped once they lived with a single payment. Ownership, not guilt about money, tracked with wanting to continue.

    What it cannot show yet

    It was one bundled condition with no control group, so it cannot separate pricing from the single path, or either from novelty. The next study is a 2 × 2 design with usage logs over 8 to 12 weeks.

    CHAPTER 09The research paper

    The study, written up for CHI.

    Submitted to ACM CHI 2027 · under review

    One Payment, One Path: Pricing and Structural Curation as a Joint Commitment Device in Self Directed Language Learning

    A mixed methods pilot (n = 20) of Germanly. Confidence and motivation rose, tool use consolidated, and learners who expected subscriptions to motivate them more judged the opposite after living with one payment. Psychological ownership, not sunk cost guilt, correlated with continuing.
